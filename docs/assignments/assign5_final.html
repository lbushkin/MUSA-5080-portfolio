Background: The goal of our model:

Chicago has long faced housing affordability challenges, and home prices vary considerably across the city’s neighborhoods. Understanding what drives these differences is essential to understanding the local housing market. In this project, we examine the factors influencing single-family home sale prices in Chicago from 2023 to 2025 and develop a predictive model to estimate prices for properties without recent sales. This model can help us explore how property characteristics, proximity to amenities, and location shape home values and provide insights into the challenges of equitable property assessment. We want to focus on the phenomena of “The Tax Divide,” meaning whether lower-value homes were assessed at higher shares of their worth (and are over-taxed) than expensive one in Chicago.

check_na <- function(formula, df) {
  vars <- all.vars(formula)
  bad <- vars[sapply(vars, function(v) any(is.na(df[[v]])))]
  if (length(bad) > 0) {
    stop("These variables have NAs: ", paste(bad, collapse = ", "),
         ". Fill them or choose another variable (Rule 1).")
  }
}


cv_rmse <- function(formula, data) {
  df <- if (inherits(data, "sf")) st_drop_geometry(data) else data
  check_na(formula, df)
  set.seed(5080)
  fit <- train(formula, data = df, method = "lm",
               trControl = trainControl(method = "cv", number = 10))
  fit$results$RMSE
}


cv_pred <- function(formula, data) {
  df <- if (inherits(data, "sf")) st_drop_geometry(data) else data
  check_na(formula, df)
  set.seed(5080)
  fit <- train(formula, data = df, method = "lm",
               trControl = trainControl(method = "cv", number = 10,
                                        savePredictions = "final"))
  fit$pred %>% arrange(rowIndex) %>% pull(pred)
}


socrata_get <- function(dataset, where, select = NULL, limit = 200000) {
  where <- str_squish(where)
  q <- paste0("$where=", URLencode(where, reserved = TRUE), "&$limit=", limit)
  if (!is.null(select)) q <- paste0(q, "&$select=", URLencode(str_squish(select), reserved = TRUE))
  url <- paste0("https://data.cityofchicago.org/resource/", dataset, ".geojson?", q)
  tmp <- tempfile(fileext = ".geojson")
  resp <- tryCatch(download.file(url, tmp, quiet = TRUE), error = function(e) e)
  if (inherits(resp, "error")) stop("Portal request failed. Open this URL in a browser to see why:\n", url)
  st_read(tmp, quiet = TRUE)
}

Part 1: Clean and Explore

We first examined the dataset, focusing on variables related to the physical characteristics of each house. To better understand how property characteristics differ across price levels, we divided homes into three categories based on sale price quartiles: low-priced homes (bottom 25%), medium-priced homes (middle 50%), and high-priced homes (top 25%).

Our initial spatial analysis revealed a clear geographic pattern: low-priced homes are concentrated primarily in the South Side of Chicago, while high-priced homes are more prevalent in the northern part of the city, particularly near Lake Michigan. This pattern suggested that location may play an important role in determining home sale prices, motivating us to investigate tract level and neighborhood level factors in our predictive model.

Part 2: Spatial Variable Additions

We then considered how spatial variables may help to better indicate the value of a house.

Distance to L

Since the L is one of the public transit options that connects different parts of Chicago, we believed it was important to include the nearest distance to an L station in our model. We found that the further a home is from an L station, the lower the sale price. Because it does not fully follow a linear trend, we found that logging the distance to the L station improved the model.

Part 3: Final model

Building character:

  • Building size has a strong positive association with sale price: each additional square foot is associated with an estimated $316 increase in price.
  • Lot size is also positively associated with sale price.
  • Building age has a nonlinear relationship with price, as indicated by the positive squared-age coefficient and negative linear-age coefficient.
  • Bedroom count also matters: compared with the house that have 1-2 bedroom, homes with 3 bedrooms and 4–5 bedrooms have lower estimated prices, while homes with 6 or more bedrooms have higher estimated prices.

Spatial character:

  • Distance to the CBD has a significant negative coefficient, suggesting that homes farther from downtown tend to have lower prices.
  • Distance to outdoor seating also has a negative association with sale price, although the estimated effect is relatively small per unit of distance.
  • Crime counts within 500 feet are negatively associated with home prices.

Neighborhood socioeconomic characteristics:’

  • The percentage of residents with a bachelor’s degree has a nonlinear relationship with sale price: the linear coefficient is negative and the squared term is positive. This may reflect the fact that an area with a very high share of residents with a Bachelor’s degree may be areas where there are lots of graduate students, and therefore cannot afford to live in expensive housing.
  • Median neighborhood income and the percentage of residents aged over 64 are not statistically significant in this model. This may be because the community fixed effect accounts for these variables.

Community-level differences:

  • Many community fixed-effect coefficients are statistically significant. For example, Lincoln Park, Gold Coast, and Old Town have large positive coefficients relative to the omitted reference community, while several South and West Side communities have negative coefficients.

cv_rmse <- function(formula, data) {
  df <- if (inherits(data, "sf")) st_drop_geometry(data) else data
  check_na(formula, df)
  set.seed(5080)
  fit <- train(formula, data = df, method = "lm",
               trControl = trainControl(method = "cv", number = 10))
  fit$results$RMSE
}


cv_pred <- function(formula, data) {
  df <- if (inherits(data, "sf")) st_drop_geometry(data) else data
  check_na(formula, df)
  set.seed(5080)
  fit <- train(formula, data = df, method = "lm",

                                        savePredictions = "final"))

}

## Part 6: Reflection 

1. **Read one coefficient twice:** once as β̂, once for what it does to ŷ.

In our final model, the coefficient for the share of people over 64 is 141.9078 (β). Therefore for every 1% increase in the share of elderly people in a census tract, there is a $141.91 increase in predicted sale price of a home, holding all other variables constant.


2. **Would you hand this model to the Assessor?** What would you warn them about?

Our model's findings are consistent with the Chicago Tribune's investigation, which found that lower-value homes were assessed at a higher proportion of their actual value than more expensive homes. In our analysis, lower-value homes had an under/overvaluation ratio of `r round(price_ratio_table$ratio[price_ratio_table$price_category == "low"], 3)`, compared with `r round(price_ratio_table$ratio[price_ratio_table$price_category == "high"], 3)` for higher-value homes. Nevertheless, we do not recommend that the Cook County Assessor adopt this model for property assessment, as further validation and evaluation of its potential biases are needed.

First, our final model is still off by $273,157.80. This is an immense range and can skew future assessments to either over- or under-value a home in drastically incorrect ways.

Second, while our final model does seem to address issues of spatial autocorrelation from our baseline model, it over-fixes by now under-valuing a much larger share of homes.

Third, these are both indications that we are "missing something" -- either demographic, spatial, or some other metric -- that would help better explain sale price. On the other hand, we may have included features that though they contributed to the overall RMSE, they may not actually be useful variables going forward. For example, distance to groceries did not lower the RMSE so much and so may not be worth including in future models.

Finally, while a tax assessment model may help people lower their taxes, if it is an open source government model, it could be used by for-profit real estate companies to "game the market" in order to aquire under-taxed homes. There is no such thing as no-strings-attached in the real estate market.
